Guitar robot concepts, v0.2

Two ways to build the chord hand, on the same guitar with the same pick hand. Both play the same song, so you can compare them directly. Real scale on a 1 cm cutting-mat grid; yellow parts are actuators and glow when energized.

Design notes

Finger grid. A barre bar plus a 6 × 3 grid of fingers on one carriage. One slide reaches every open chord and every E-shape and A-shape barre chord.

Rows follow the frets. Fret spacing shrinks about 6% per fret, so the rows are linked (drawn as a scissor linkage) and close up toward the body.

Staggered solenoids. Each solenoid is wider than the 7 mm string spacing at the nut, so neighbours sit front and back.

Position. One stepper on a lead screw, homed at the nut. The fret sensor flashes each time it passes a fret, confirming position.

Fast within a position. Open-chord changes are just solenoids switching. Only barre-position changes move the carriage, and then every finger has to lift.

One finger per string. Each finger rides its own slot, cut to follow its string. No fret window: any chord shape, any stretch, anywhere on the neck. A barre is just six fingers on the same fret.

Six motors. Each finger is pulled by its own stepper through a cable loop. Six motors don't fit on the neck, so they sit in a bank over the headstock.

Thin carriages. Strings are 7 mm apart at the nut, so each carriage and its press actuator must be under about 6 mm wide. This is the hardest constraint; the model draws them at 4 mm.

Fingers move only when they must. Notes shared between chords stay pressed and keep ringing, and lifted fingers slide into place early, while the previous chord is still sounding.

Slides, not hammer-ons. A finger that moves while pressed plays a slide, which the grid can't do. With one finger per string, there's no second finger to hammer on with.
